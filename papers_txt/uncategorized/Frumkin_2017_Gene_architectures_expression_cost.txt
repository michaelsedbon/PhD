Drugging ACAT1 for Cancer Therapy

In this issue, Fan et al. (2016) show that oncogenic tyrosine kinases can promote glycolysis by phos-
phorylating and stabilizing the tetrameric form of mitochondrial acetyl-coA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1).
The authors further identify a small molecule ACAT1 inhibitor that displays anti-cancer effects.
Oncogenic alterations modify the meta-
bolic program of cancer cells (Vander Hei-
den et al., 2009). As many cancer cells are
dependent on these changes in meta-
bolism, there has been great interest in
exploiting metabolic dependencies for
the development of new cancer-selective
therapies. Metabolic programs are in part
regulated by post-translational modiﬁca-
tions (PTMs) of enzymes and small mole-
cule transporters. These modiﬁcations
impact enzyme and transport function
by modulating protein-protein interac-
tions, changing the subcellular localiza-
tion or hindering the access of substrates
to the active site.
Among these PTMs, reversible lysine
acetylation
of
metabolic
enzymes,
including
cytoplasmic
central
carbon
metabolism enzymes such as pyruvate
kinase (PKM2) (Lv et al., 2011), lactate
dehydrogenase A (LDHA) (Zhao et al.,
2013), and ATP citrate lyase (ACLY) (Lin
et al., 2013), plays a major role in the regu-
lation of cellular metabolism
and tumor growth. Further-
more, acetylation is perhaps
most relevant in mitochon-
drial metabolism, as remark-
ably over 40% of mitochon-
drial proteins are acetylated,
cementing
its
substantial
role in cellular metabolism
(Kim et al., 2006). While the
development of rapid mass
spectrometric
technologies
identiﬁed
a
plethora
of
acetylated proteins in normal
and cancer cells, the up-
stream oncogenic regulators
for acetylation/deacetylation
cycles and the mechanism
by
which
they
inﬂuence
cellular
metabolism
remain
elusive.
Acetylation of proteins in mitochondria
might occur non-enzymatically by reac-
tive lysine residues and acetyl-CoA or
enzymatically mediated by lysine acetyl
transferases (KATs). Even though mito-
chondrial lysine deacetylases that remove
lysinemodiﬁcations are well deﬁned, there
are only few examples of mitochondrial
KATs. In their previous work, Fan et al.
identiﬁed
such
a
mitochondrial
KAT,
acetyl-coA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1),
that links oncogenic signals to energy
metabolism (Fan et al., 2014). ACAT1 is a
tetramer in its active form and catalyzes
the conversion of two acetyl-CoA mole-
cules to acetoacetyl-coA and coA but
also acts as an upstream acetyl trans-
ferase
for
pyruvate
dehydrogenase
complex, a key decision point between
glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation.
Building upon this work, in this issue of
Molecular Cell, the same group now iden-
tiﬁed how ACAT1 activity is hijacked by
oncogenic alterations, promoting glycol-
ysis and tumor growth. Remarkably, in
many cancer types, ACAT1 is phosphory-
lated by oncogenic tyrosine kinases at its
Y407 residue, resulting in the stabilization
of the active tetrameric form of the
enzyme. Upon stable tetramer formation,
ACAT1 inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase
activity through acetylation of pyruvate
dehydrogenase (PDHA1) and pyruvate
dehydrogenase phosphatase (PDP1) and
diverts pyruvate to lactate production to
regenerate reducing equivalents (NAD+)
required for continuous glycolysis and
production of biosynthetic intermediates
(Figure 1). This interesting mode of regula-
tion provides a mechanistic explanation
for
how
oncogenic
alterations
can
contribute to Warburg effect and points
out a signaling pathway inside mitochon-
dria involving EGF-stimulated kinases,
ACAT1 and PDH.
Another exciting part of this study is the
discovery of a small molecule inhibitor
that targets the active tetrameric form of
ACAT1. This covalent small
molecule inhibitor, arecoline
hydrobromide
(AH),
binds
to and disrupts ACAT1 tetra-
mers into monomers, thus
making
tyrosine
kinases
unable
to
phosphorylate
and stabilize active ACAT1
(Figure 1). Arecoline hydro-
bromide slows cancer cell
proliferation
in
vitro
and
in vivo, particularly under con-
ditions that are dependent on
decreased
PDH
ﬂux.
This
molecule adds to the growing
list of metabolic enzyme in-
hibitors that could potentially
be used in cancer therapy.

target
of

Figure 1. Oncogenic Activation of ACAT1 Rewires Cancer Cell
Metabolism and Is Targeted by a Small Molecule Inhibitor
Oncogenic tyrosine kinases phosphorylate and stabilize the tetrameric form of
ACAT1, which in turn inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity and di-
verts pyruvate into lactate and NAD+ production. While ACAT1 is a metabolic
enzyme involved in ketone and amino acid metabolism, it is also a mito-
chondrial lysine acetyl transferase for PDH. Targeting ACAT1 with a small
molecule inhibitor, arecoline hydrobromide, disrupts ACAT1 tetramers into
inactive monomers, derepresses its inhibitory effect on PDH, and has anti-
cancer effects.

development in the last decade. In partic-
ular, several inhibitors (i.e., dichloroacetic
acid [DCA] and AZD7545) targeting pyru-
vate dehydrogenase kinase, a negative
regulator of PDH complex, have recently
been developed and tested in preclinical
and clinical settings with some success
(Dunbar et al., 2014). In the future, it will
be important to determine how ACAT1 in-
hibition will differ from PDK inhibitors that
are already in clinical trials and identify pa-
tient populations that will beneﬁt the most
from either approach.
It has recently been suggested that
acetyl-coA availability in different cellular
compartments might inﬂuence acetylation
status of proteins and may be a rate-
limiting factor for the activity of KATs
(Pietrocola
et
al.,
2015).
Additionally,
compartment-speciﬁc deacetylases such
as SIRT3 that negatively regulate acetyla-
tion in mitochondria are dependent on
NAD+ availability (Onyango et al., 2002).
Both acetyl-coA and NAD+ levels change
in response to metabolic stress condi-
tions and are likely additional determi-
nants for ACAT1-dependent regulation
of pyruvate dehydrogenase activity. Un-
fortunately, mitochondrial levels of these
metabolites, particularly in different can-
cer types, have not yet been carefully
measured particularly in vivo. With the
advance of recent compartment-speciﬁc
metabolomics technologies (Chen et al.,
2016), it might be possible to determine
how concentrations of these metabolites
in mitochondria change and inﬂuence
acetylation-mediated regulation of energy
metabolism in response to metabolic
stress conditions (i.e., glucose deprivation
and hypoxia) observed in tumors. It should
be noted that, unlike PDH, ACAT1 is also a
key enzyme in ketone and amino acid
metabolismandtheeffectofACAT1inhibi-
tion on cancer growth may be partly medi-
ated by its function in ketone and amino
acid metabolism. Future work is required
to dissect how these additional metabolic
pathways contribute to cancer formation
and whether there is any synergy for inhib-
iting both functions of ACAT1.
