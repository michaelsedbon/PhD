Abstract
Germinal centers host a mini-evolutionary environment where B cells can mutate their receptor and be
selected depending on its afﬁnity to target antigens in a process called afﬁnity maturation. Starting from
founder cells with a weak B cell receptor afﬁnity, germinal centers release output cells as antibody-secreting
cells or memory cells with a very high afﬁnity, a property which is essential for pathogen clearance and
immune memory. Therapeutic interventions on the germinal centers are tantalizing approaches to improve
vaccines or to support rejection of chronic pathogens such as HIV. However, the complexity of the
selection processes makes it very hard to make reliable predictions. Here, we present in detail how to
build an agent-based model (hyphasma), accounting for the dynamics of the germinal center. It encom-
passes the core quantitative traits of afﬁnity maturation, and allowed to make reliable predictions in previous
studies.
Key words Germinal center, Afﬁnity maturation, Agent-based modeling, Somatic hypermutation

Introduction: Spatial and Functional Structure of Germinal Centers
During the formation of a Germinal Center (GC), two zones
emerge, the dark zone and the light zone (Fig. 1). While the steps
leading to this characteristic structure are still poorly understood, the
events happening in both of them have been extensively character-
ized and are critical for a proper afﬁnity maturation of antibodies.
The dark zone contains stromal cells producing CXCL12 and
proliferating B cells named centroblasts. The clonal expansion of
centroblasts is accompanied by somatic hypermutation (SHM)
speciﬁcally targeting the receptor (BCR), and consequently the
generation of further lineages with diverse afﬁnities towards the
antigen. Cutting-edge single-cell sequencing methods now allow
to characterize lineages generated from the same founder clone as
harboring the same VDJ recombination pattern, together with
their mutation history, shedding light on the diversity of mutations
happening in one GC in vivo [1].

The light zone, however, is composed of nondividing B cells

mal network of Follicular Dendritic Cells (FDCs) producing
CXCL13 and carrying antigens in their native structure on immune
complexes bound via complement Fc receptors. Centrocytes with
high afﬁnity to an antigen can bind to it, receive BCR signaling
necessary for their survival, and capture this antigen. Finally, cen-
trocytes that successfully captured an antigen can process and fur-
ther present it on their MHC class II, thereby acting as an Antigen
Presenting Cell (APC) for the T helper cells present in the GC,
called T Follicular Helper (Tfh) cells. The interaction between Tfhs
and centrocytes further gives bidirectional signals, leading to the
proliferation of the B cell together with the maintenance of the
phenotype of the Tfh [2]. Interestingly, it has been shown that the
antigen carried by B cells is not always equally distributed among
daughter cells upon division [3], raising the possibility of B cell fate
decision being based on the amount of carried antigen [4].
The GC is a very dynamic structure. B cells and T cells con-
stantly migrate on the static network of stromal cells and FDCs.

The selection pressure on B cells emerges from a combination
of processes, among which are the abundance or frequency of
antigens, the capacity to take an antigen depending on the BCR
afﬁnity, and the chance to ﬁnd a Tfh. It is therefore not straightfor-

efﬁcient afﬁnity maturation, and even harder to make predictions

porates all these effects, and allowed to investigate their relative
contribution and to predict or support new ﬁndings.

2.2
Representation
of Antigens and
Receptors
The distribution of antigens in the GC is still poorly understood.
Following infection, antibodies are produced with high afﬁnity
towards speciﬁc epitopes of the pathogen’s antigens, but it is still
not clear whether each GC carries mainly one antigen and supports
preferentially directed afﬁnity maturation towards this antigen, or if
each GC carries a large diversity of antigens. In our model, we take
the assumption that only one antigen per GC, as a ﬁrst approach.
Regarding the receptor speciﬁcity, another interesting property
of the GC is the cross-checking of B cells by T cells, which is likely
to avoid nonspeciﬁc activation of B cells that could lead to autoim-
munity [10]. If a B cell response is initiated without the activation
of T cells, no proper GC is formed, and only few antibodies are
produced [11]. Further, if T cells are depleted during the course of
a GC, the GC is shown to shrink and terminate [12], most likely
because centrocytes do not receive a rescue signal then.
From a mechanistic point of view of this cross-checking, B cells
need to internalize antigen, process it and present it to the Tfh,
implying that the epitopes seen by the Tfh are different than the real
3D epitopes of the native antigen as it can be bound by the B cells.
Therefore, even if a B cell is speciﬁc to a particular antigen epitope,
it can most probably be rescued by several Tfh that can recognize
different parts of the antigen, as presented on the MHC. Here, we
assume that all the Tfh cells that could enter the GC recognize a
sub-peptide of the antigen and therefore can support the selection
of all B cells, provided the B cells present enough antigen. Another
way to put it is that we only simulate Tfhs that can support B cells,

The characteristic structure of the GC relies on chemotaxis, and
therefore the dynamic expression of chemokine receptors by T cells
and B cells. Chemotaxis to CXCL12 and CXCL13 applies to cells
expressing CXCR4 and CXCR5, respectively. While all cells in the
GC express CXCR5 with a limited difference between centroblasts
and centrocytes [15], the expression of CXCR4 is dynamic and
restricted to centroblasts. We assume here that CXCR4 induced
chemotaxis is dominant over CXCR5, and that, in dependence of
CXCR4 expression, the cells will be sensitive to CXCL12 or
CXCL13.
In the model, the chemotactic capacity of cells is represented as
a Boolean property:
isResponsive2signals[CXCL12], meaning that it expresses CXCR4 (dominates over CXCR5
expression)
isResponsive2Signals[CXCL13], meaning it is CXCR4- and follows CXCL13 due to CXCR5
expression
Additionally, we include a dynamic modulation of the signal-
ing: a high local dose of chemokine allows for the desensitization of
the receptors, while resensitization can occur when the dose
becomes low enough again. The decision of sensitivity to signals
falls into two procedures: one which initiates the proper receptors
when a cell is changing phenotype, and another function, called at
each time-step, that updates the level of the receptor depending on
the current level of chemokine (see Algorithm 2).

Following a Polarity
First, each type of cell has a speciﬁc velocity (see parameter values in
Subheading 22.2.12), which is translated into a probability of
moving of a gridpoint size per time-step. Cells do not move at
each time-step, but rather fast cells move more often, to recapitu-
late a correct observed speed.
To perform a move in the grid according to the (ﬂoating point)
polarity, the new (ﬂoating point) wanted position is computed, and
the list of free neighboring positions is sorted based on their
distance with the target. One of the equally best positions is chosen
randomly, provided it does not go against the direction of the
polarity. It allows cells to follow a direction during multiple time-
steps and to walk around obstacles if the best position is not
available. If no suitable position is found, the cell stays where it was.
In the speciﬁc case that two neighboring cells intend to go in
the opposite direction (towards each other), and therefore could
not move, the movement procedure is extended by allowing
swapping of two such cells. It is described in the main loop in
Algorithm 10 and is performed by storing the list of cells that
could not move. Each such cell is allowing to swap with a neighbor
with a probability 50%. This 50% is used because the neighbor cell
might also be in the list (and will also be considered), or alternately,
if not in the list, then only the ﬁrst cell should have moved. Swap-
ping would move the other cell for free, increasing the total dis-
placement of cells of 2. dx.
Deﬁning a New Polarity
When a new polarity needs to be computed, at the end of the persis-
tence time, it is computed based on a random movement deﬁned by a
turning angle from the previous polarity and is further inﬂuenced by
chemotaxis. The turning angle distribution is directly taken from
experimental measurements [16] while the chemotactic attraction
to CXCL12 and CXCL13 is performed by adding a small contribu-
tion in the direction to the gradient of both signals. The weight
between chemotaxis and random move is supposed to be dependent
on the strength of the gradient. Details on how chemotaxis was
included in the model can be found in [17]. Additionally, the prefer-
entiallocalizationof Tfhsinto the light zone is done bya preference to
follow a direction called ‘north’ here, and pointing to it.

2.6

at the Centrocyte
Stage
Antigen Collection
Centrocytes start at the state of unselected and are looking for
interactions with FDCs and the antigens they carry, while they
have a high probability to die by apoptosis. We assume here that
the antigen collection phase has a predeﬁned time-window, and
that, at each interaction with an FDC position, the antigen is taken
with a probability directly depending on the BCR afﬁnity. In this
way, centrocytes with a high-afﬁne BCR can internalize a huge
amount of antigen compared to low afﬁnity ones. As a ﬁrst step of
selection, B cells that could not capture any antigen during the
collection period are killed. Additionally, we assume that cells leav-
ing an interaction with an FDC (successful or not) are not able to
interact again and look for antigen for a small refractory time
testDelay [18]. This avoids repeatedly testing on the same FDC
and limits the maximum amount of antigen that can be collected.

T-cell Help
When the antigen collection period is ﬁnished, centrocytes carry a
number of internalized antigens (nFDCcontacts) and need to ﬁnd a
Tfh to receive a proliferation signal. When a B cell ﬁnds a neighbor
Tfh, we assume that it requires sustained signaling for a long time in

the B cell interacts with T cells and by integrating the received
signal. However, since a Tfh cell might have different centrocyte
neighbors, we assume that only the neighbor with the highest
amount of antigen will receive the signal. Finally, if the T cell is
not rescued by Tfh help in a deﬁned time-window, it dies by
apoptosis. The differentiation decision (to recycle as centroblast
or to become an output) is delayed by a differentiation time called
individualDiffDelay here. It is important to maintain a proper light
zone to dark zone ratio of cells in simulations.

The transitions between cell stages require a few adjustments
(Algorithm 8).
First, when a cell becomes an output cell, we suppose it
becomes insensitive to chemokines. Optionally, the “move” proce-
dure can be extended to give a “south polarity” (as opposite to
Tfh), to target them preferentially to the dark zone before leaving
the GC.
When a centrocyte recycles to centroblasts, we took the
hypothesis of a dynamic mutation rate according to the afﬁnity of
a B cell (quantiﬁed as the amount of antigen it could internalize in
the light zone), which was also observed in [7]. Since the mechan-
isms involved are not fully quantiﬁed, this dependency is simulated
by a Hill function.

interactions impacts on the number of divisions that a B cell will

Initialization
To start a simulation, the location of the cells and the initial
distribution of signals have to be deﬁned. The 3D grid is initialized
to be of size N  N  N. The points which are not in a central
sphere of radius N/2 representing the GC are considered as
unavailable for cells (outside). All positions within this sphere are
initially marked with type ¼ empty and ID ¼ 1.
The initial cells are put randomly in free positions. The dark
zone is deﬁned as positions with Grid[x ¼ 1. . N, y ¼ 1. . N, z >
N/2], and a predeﬁned number of stromal cells are distributed in
the dark zone. Similarly, FDCs are randomly distributed in the light
zone.
The FDCs are multi-fragment cells, meaning that they possess
branches that can cover multiple positions in the grid. Here, they
are designed as straight branches in each direction, of length Den-
driteLength, and each position occupied by an FDC is called a
fragment. The molecules of the antigen are dispatched from the
beginning and equally distributed inside the FDCs fragments. The
branches are transparent in the sense that other cells may occupy a
position of the branch. This is performed by storing an additional
information in the grid, namely FDClisti, meaning the index of the
FDC that has a branch at that point.
Recently, the number of GC founder cells was determined and
was found in the range of 100 clones [1]. Therefore, two variants of
initialization are implemented in hyphasma: Either 3 founder
clones are predeﬁned in the beginning and divide and mutate 12
times in the expansion phase of the GC. In an alternative model, the
GC simulation starts from 0 founder clones and founder cells
continuously enter the GC reaction for a few days (Following
Algorithm 9, lines 36–47 [1]), each of them dividing six times. In
both variants, the quality, i.e., the initial afﬁnity, of the founder cells
can be restricted and adapted to the described experiment. For
example, in some transgenic experiments, the quality of the
founder cells is substantially higher than in experiments based on
wildtype germline B cell stimulations. In the latter case, a realistic
starting quality is in the range of a Hamming distance of 4–8.

Additionally, the possibility of antibody feedback, a process by
which antibodies can diffuse back to a GC and cover the antigens
at the surface of the FDCs, can be incorporated [20]. It consists
into allowing antibodies to bind antigens on fragments of FDCs,

Main Simulation
Now that each process and event has been described separately,
together with the data structure suitable for describing a GC state,
we depict the time-evolution of our GC in silico in Algorithm 10.
At each time-point, each kind of cell is updated according to its
properties.
Stromal cells and FDCs do not move but secrete chemokines.
Antibodies produced by output producers can bind antigens on the
FDCs and create immune complexes, allowing for a negative anti-
body feedback. Output cells move. The chemokines are ﬁnally
diffused according to the time-step and the diffusion constants

The state of centroblasts and centrocytes is updated and can
lead to the decision of division, death, differentiation, or recycling.

reverse directions (exchange their position) are allowed, to reduce
the effect of the discretization of the space into a grid.

Theory prediction
Using the current knowledge in order to
develop a consistent theory of GC reactions.
Example: It was generally believed that B cells
compete for antigen. The code presented here
was used in order to distinguish eight different
selection mechanisms [18]. It was shown that
competition for antigen is not robust and less
efﬁcient for afﬁnity maturation than other
mechanisms. In hyphasma, the most efﬁcient
mechanism was competition for T cell help.
This prediction was supported 4 years later by
the ﬁnding that B cells got signals for recycling
to the dark zone phenotype and division [15]. A
quantitative analysis of the B cell expansion
found in this experiment induced the prediction
that the number of divisions is regulated by Tfh
in an afﬁnity-dependent manner between 1 and
6 divisions [8]. This prediction was proven true
2 years later [7] and further implications could
be drawn from this mechanism [6].
Theory distinction
Testing of various hypotheses for consistency
with a particular data set.
Example: Experiments that suppress or know-
out a particular part of the GC reaction may lead
to a complex phenotype involving several read-
outs and may also include subtle differences.
Such a complex phenotype does not allow to
determine the impact point of the suppressed
molecule because the interdependency of the
different modulated read-outs is unclear. For
example, the suppression of the microRNA-
155 generated such a complex phenotype with
changed B cell population dynamics, afﬁnity
maturation, fraction of apoptotic cells, and
amount of generated output cells. Hyphasma
was used to test six different models of how
microRNA-155 would impact on the GC

Philippe A. Robert et al.

reaction assuming that the whole phenotype
stems from a single impact point. In this way,
two of the six tested hypotheses were found
compatible with the phenotype, namely a defect
in BCR signaling as well as increased apoptosis
of positively selected B cells [23]. This result
focused further experimentation onto these
two mechanisms. Similar applications were
done with hyphasma in the context of the
impact of TLR-4 on GCs [24] and of sequential
switching to the IgE isotype [25].
While the development of a code for a particular biological
system can take several years, once it is setup, it has a great potential
of interpreting experiments and generating novel theories. We
believe that the fundamental reason that it worked out in the case
of hyphasma and the GC reaction is a strict mechanistic implemen-
tation on the cellular scale. Subcellular processes were implemented
in a phenomenological way and nothing but the boundary condi-
tions were implemented on the GC scale. Only then, the appear-
ance of the GC in silico can be tracked back to the mechanisms that
made this appearance emerge. It is this principle of scale integrity
that makes the difference between a reproductive simulation and a
predictive theory.
