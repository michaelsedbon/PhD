Abstract-The 
generation of catalytic antibodies should enable the catalysis of reactions for which 
no enzymatic or chemical catalyst is currently available. In previous 
studies. we established 
a series 
of catalytic 
antibodies 
capable 
of hydrolysing 
p-nitrobenzyl 
(pNB) and p-nitrophenyl 
(pNP) esters. 
A group 
of these catalytic 
antibodies 
exhibited 
high reactivity 
and substrate 
specificity, 
yet each 
individual 
antibody 
demonstrated 
different 
kinetic parameters. 
In order to study the molecular 
basis 
for these differences. 
we have cloned, sequenced 
and expressed 
the variable 
regions of this group of 
antibodies 
as functional 
scFv and Fv in bacteria. 
The variable 
region of the heavy chain is derived 
from a novel germline 
gene of the J558 family whereas 
the light chain comes from a germline 
gene 
previously 
found 
in our catalytic 
antibodies 
catalysing 
the hydrolysis 
of only nitrophenyl 
esters, 
demonstrating 
that the heavy chain determines 
the specificity 
for the nitrobenzyl 
esters. 
Several 
different expression 
systems were examined 
for their ability to produce catalytically 
active antibodies. 
When expressed 
as an scFv, both refolded 
and secreted 
scFvs exhibited 
catalytic 
activity although 
yields of expressed 
protein were low. The secreted scFvs had higher specific activity. On the other hand, 
Fv fragments 
were expressed 
in sufficient quantities 
to allow kinetic analysis. Levels of expression 
were 
dependent 
on the sequence of V, used. Using this expression 
system, the relative contributions 
of the 
individual 
light and heavy chains to catalysis 
and binding could be evaluated. 
Both original V, and 
V,_ regions are required for hapten binding, although 
the VH is more crucial for catalysis. 
By replacing 
the CDR3 of the heavy chain with a random 
sequence, 
it was shown to be essential for both binding 
and catalysis. 
This expression 
system together 
with site-directed 
mutagenesis 
should enable a more 
detailed 
study of the catalytic 
mechanism 
of this set of antibodies.

INTRODUCTION 
The idea that antibodies 
with affinity for transition 
states 
would show enzyme-like 
behavior 
was first postulated 
by 
Jencks (I 969), and led to the birth of a new experimental 
field of catalytic 
antibodies. 
Since the first reports 
of 
catalytic 
antibodies 
by Pollack 
et al. (1986) and Tra- 
montano 
et al. (1986), the major focus of research in this 
field has been to define the scope of chemical 
reactions 
that can be subjected 
to catalysis 
by antibodies 
(Guo et 
al., 1995). 
Catalytic 
antibodies 
provide 
a strategy 
for generating 
new catalysts 
for reactions 
or specificities 
difficult 
to 
achieve 
by existing 
enzymic 
or chemical 
methods.

of the parental 
antibody

they have been 
derived

et al., 1988). The first 
construction 
and characterization 
of catalytic 
scFv was 
reported 
by Gibbs 
et al. (1991). This protein 
was expre- 
ssed in E. coli in inclusion 
bodies and showed the same 
catalytic 
parameters 
as the parent 
monoclonal 
antibody 
after refolding. 
Later, this scFv protein 
was expressed 
in 
soluble 
form by modification 
of the vector 
(Stewart 
et 
al.. 1994).

state analog 
(TSA) p-nitrobenzyl

(lb; designations

efficiently 
hydrolyse 
both the p-nitrobenzyl 
(2b) and p- 
nitrophenyl 
(2~) esters (Tawfik et al., 1997a) in addition 
to binding 
to their 
respective 
phosphonate 
esters. 
A 
group of these monoclonal 
antibodies 
has been found to 
display one of the highest esterase activities demonstrated 
thus far. In this study, 
the variable 
regions 
of catalytic 
antibodies 
D2.3, D2.4 and D2.5 were sequenced, 
cloned 
and expressed as scFv and Fv in E. coli. and their enzymic 
behavior 
was evaluated. 
When 
the sequences 
of these 
antibodies 
were compared 
to those of antibodies 
specific 
for nitrophenyl 
ester hydrolysis 
(Zemel 
et al.. 1994), it 
can be seen that whereas the light chains are derived from 
the same germline 
gene. the variable 
region of the heavy 
chain is from a different 
5558 germline 
family member. 
Chain-swapping 
experiments 
show that both chains are 
necessary 
for both the binding 
and catalytic 
activities; 
however, 
catalytic 
properties 
are mainly 
affected by the 
heavy chain.

An alternative procedure for expressing Fv fragments

chains separately in the same cell and to rely on their

ssed as a soluble fragment in the periplasm and culture

lesser degree. Most of it appeared as inclusion bodies 
like the corresponding 
D2.4-3 scFv (data not shown). 
Following affinity-purification 
by TSA-agarose column 
from the periplasmic extract, the final yield of Fv was 
higher than 2mg/l culture and the purified Fvs showed 
binding and catalysis (Fig. 6C,D). SDS-PAGE 
analysis 
of purified Fvs showed a major band with two minor 
bands, one of higher and the other of lower molecular 
weight (Fig. 7, bottom). The calculated A4, of V, and VL 
is 13 900 and 14 100 respectively, giving practically similar 
mobilities on SDS-PAGE. In the Western blot, the lowest 
band was not detected by anti-E tag antibody and can be 
therefore regarded as the VL that has lost the E tag, 
similarly to that observed in scFv. The highest band 
could, most likely, represent VH and/or VL which retain 
the leader peptide as well as the E tag of VL. Since we 
identified the protein in the immunoblot 
by anti-E tag 
antibody and the E tag is fused only to the V,, the possi- 
bility exists that the active species purified by the affinity 
column contains only the light chain. This has been ruled 
out in the experiments described below. In contrast to 
scFv, the Fv did not aggregate upon concentration, which 
was a significant factor in obtaining concentrations 
of 
protein high enough to measure catalytic activity.

DISCUSSION 
We have cloned and expressed in E. coli as Fv frag- 
ments the variable regions of a series of antibodies cat- 
alysing hydrolysis of the p-nitrobenzyl and p-nitrophenyl 
esters. The light chains are from the Vti2 family. Inter- 
estingly, group I of another group of six catalytic anti- 
bodies derived from immunization with a p-nitrophenyl 
TSA (Zemel et al., 1994) which we divided into two 
groups based on their light chain usage also used the light 
chain found in the D2.x series. The VK~ family (Strohal 
et al., 1989) contains about six germline genes, four of 
which are functional (Kofler et al., 1992; Schupp et al., 
1997). All of these catalytic light chains are derived from 
the same germline gene, Vk2(70/1), a germline gene not 
commonly used. Of the nine sequences in the Genbank 
database (Release 97, 10/96) using this germline gene, 
four represent the catalytic antibodies described by us.
