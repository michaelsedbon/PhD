## An orthogonal T7 replisome for continuous hypermutation and accelerated evolution in E. coli
**File:** `An_orthogonal_T7_replisome_for.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.adp9583?utm_source=chatgpt.com

This paper introduces T7-ORACLE, an orthogonal T7 replisome-based system in *E. coli* for continuous in vivo hypermutation and accelerated directed evolution. The authors engineer T7 DNA polymerase variants to achieve mutation rates ~100,000-fold above background, specifically on circular plasmid templates replicated by the orthogonal replisome. The system avoids host genome mutagenesis and integrates into standard laboratory workflows with high transformation efficiencies. As a proof of concept, the authors evolve TEM-1 β-lactamase for enhanced activity against cefotaxime, achieving a substantial increase in resistance within days. T7-ORACLE represents a major step toward scalable, hands-free continuous evolution.

## Orthogonal replication with optogenetic selection evolves yeast JEN1 into a mevalonate transporter
**File:** `Orthogonal_replication_with_optogenetic_selection.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.embopress.org/doi/full/10.1038/s44320-025-00113-5

This paper introduces OptoRep, a system combining continuous directed evolution (via OrthoRep) with optogenetic selection to fine-tune selection pressure. The authors engineer a mevalonate-auxotrophic yeast strain with light-controlled growth and evolve the yeast JEN1 monocarboxylate transporter into a mevalonate importer — a gain-of-function mutation. Light-tunable selection allowed gradual increases in stringency, facilitating the evolution of mutations that would be inaccessible under constant strong selection. The evolved transporter improved farnesene production. The work demonstrates how combining orthogonal mutagenesis with optogenetic selection enables the evolution of challenging gain-of-function phenotypes.

## Light inducible protein degradation in E. coli with LOVtag
**File:** `Light_inducible_protein_degradation_in.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.02.25.530042v1.full.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com

This paper introduces LOVtag, a modular optogenetic tag for light-inducible protein degradation in *E. coli*. When fused to a target protein, LOVtag triggers degradation upon blue light exposure via the endogenous Lon and ClpXP protease systems. The system is highly modular — demonstrated across transcription factors, metabolic enzymes, and efflux pumps — and can be combined with other optogenetic tools for multi-layer control. Applications include dynamic metabolic engineering (e.g., toggling enzyme levels to optimize product yield) and studying gene function through rapid protein depletion. The paper also characterizes the frequency-response behavior of LOVtag for tunable protein level control.

## Engineering an allosteric transcription factor to respond to new ligands
**File:** `Engineering_an_allosteric_transcription_factor.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.3696

This paper presents a strategy for engineering allosteric transcription factors (aTFs) to sense new ligands. Using the *E. coli* LacI repressor as a model, the authors combine computational ligand docking, single-residue saturation mutagenesis of the ligand-binding pocket, and error-prone PCR to produce LacI variants responsive to fucose, gentiobiose, lactitol, and sucralose. Importantly, the newly engineered specificities retain proper allosteric regulation (i.e., ligand binding still induces the conformational change that derepresses transcription). The approach establishes a general framework for creating custom biosensors and regulatory switches for metabolic engineering and synthetic biology.

## Optogenetic Control of Microbial Consortia Populations for Chemical Production
**File:** `Optogenetic_Control_of_Microbial_Consortia.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems, optogenetic T7DNAp
**URL:** https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acssynbio.1c00182

This paper demonstrates optogenetic control of microbial consortium composition for improved chemical production. The authors develop the OptoTA circuit, which uses the MazEF toxin-antitoxin system under blue light control to regulate *E. coli* growth rate. By adjusting light duty cycles, they dynamically tune the population ratio in *E. coli*–*S. cerevisiae* co-cultures, improving production of isobutyl acetate and naringenin. The approach highlights optogenetics as a powerful, non-chemical tool for dynamic population control in consortia-based biomanufacturing, avoiding the limitations of chemical inducers that can interfere with cell metabolism or product composition.

## Colicin Biology
**File:** `Colicin_Biology.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://journals.asm.org/doi/full/10.1128/mmbr.00036-06?utm_source=chatgpt.com

This comprehensive review covers all aspects of colicin biology — from synthesis, release, and structural organization to receptor binding, import across the outer and inner membranes, and cytotoxic mechanisms. Colicins are protein antibiotics produced by *E. coli* that kill closely related strains via pore formation or nuclease activity. The review details the Tol- and TonB-dependent translocation pathways, the molecular basis of immunity protein protection, and the evolutionary ecology of colicin-mediated bacterial competition. It also discusses colicin-like bacteriocins from other genera and applications of colicins as laboratory tools, including biosensing and protein purification.

## Nuclease colicins and their immunity proteins
**File:** `Nuclease_colicins_and_their_immunity.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems, Colicin
**URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/quarterly-reviews-of-biophysics/article/abs/nuclease-colicins-and-their-immunity-proteins/896D3E88370BBA45D1B37357B1171D0F?utm_source=chatgpt.com

This detailed review focuses specifically on nuclease colicins — enzymatic bacteriocins that kill *E. coli* by cleaving DNA, rRNA, or tRNA — and their cognate immunity (Im) proteins. It covers the modular structure of colicins (translocation, receptor-binding, and cytotoxic domains), the import pathway through outer and inner membranes, and the molecular mechanisms of nuclease activity. The paper also reviews immunity protein function, the kinetics and thermodynamics of the ultra-high-affinity ColE–Im interaction (Kd ≤ 10⁻¹⁴ M), dual-recognition specificity mechanisms, and co-evolutionary dynamics. Additionally, it discusses Im protein folding and the use of colicin–Im systems as models for studying protein–protein interaction design and evolution.

## Studying SARS-CoV-2 interactions using phage-displayed receptor binding domain as a model protein
**File:** `Studying_SARS-CoV-2_interactions_using_phage-displayed.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-50450-4

This paper uses phage display to present the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain (RBD) on filamentous phage surfaces, enabling high-throughput mutational scanning. By constructing RBD mutant libraries and selecting for binding to ACE2 or neutralizing antibodies, the system identifies mutations that enhance or reduce binding. The approach provides a platform for mapping functional epitopes and predicting escape mutations. The study demonstrates that phage-displayed RBD retains functional folding and binding properties, making it a useful model for studying protein–protein interactions and for rapid characterization of emerging viral variants.

## SARS-CoV-2 variant prediction and antiviral drug design are enabled by RBD in vitro evolution
**File:** `SARS-CoV-2_variant_prediction_and_antiviral.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-021-00954-4

This study uses yeast surface display and iterative in vitro evolution to affinity-mature the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD for ACE2 binding. Strikingly, the mutations selected in the lab (S477N, E484K, N501Y) match those found in real-world variants of concern (Alpha, Beta, Gamma), demonstrating that in vitro evolution can predict viral evolution. The best variant, RBD-62, achieves 1,000-fold higher ACE2 affinity (16 pM) than wild-type. RBD-62 functions as a competitive inhibitor of ACE2, blocking SARS-CoV-2 infection in vitro and reducing disease in a hamster challenge model. A 2.9 Å cryo-EM structure reveals the structural basis of enhanced binding.

## SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibody structures inform therapeutic strategies
**File:** `SARS-CoV-2_neutralizing_antibody_structures_inform.txt`
**Subjects:** HyperMutation Systems
**URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2852-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com

This structural biology study classifies SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) into four classes based on their binding modes to the spike RBD. Class 1 (VH3-53, short CDRH3) blocks ACE2 and binds only "up" RBDs. Class 2 blocks ACE2 but binds both up and down RBDs, with some bridging adjacent RBDs — notably, C144 locks the spike into a closed conformation via a hydrophobic CDRH3 tip. Class 3 binds outside the ACE2 site on both up and down RBDs. Class 4 binds a conserved cryptic epitope on up RBDs only. Mutant mapping shows escape mutations from one class don't affect others, supporting therapeutic cocktail strategies combining NAbs from different classes.
